# IB Business Management SL — Formulae and Definitions

Everything with an equals sign, plus the terms that actually get asked in "define the term" questions. Items marked **HL** are outside the SL course — they're here so you can skip them knowingly.

> **You are given a formulae sheet in Paper 2.** What you are *not* given is the layout of the profit and loss account, the balance sheet, or the cash-flow forecast. Those you must reproduce from memory — which is why the formats matter more than the formulas.

Two things the formula sheet still can't do for you: tell you *which* formula the question wants, and tell you which profit figure goes into it. Learn these well enough that you never open the sheet, and you buy yourself minutes for the 10-marker.

# Part one — Every equation

## Costs and revenue

```
Total revenue        = price × quantity sold
Total variable costs = variable cost per unit × quantity
Total costs          = total fixed costs + total variable costs
Profit               = total revenue − total costs
```

## Contribution and break-even

```
Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit
Total contribution    = contribution per unit × quantity
                      = total revenue − total variable costs
 
Profit                = total contribution − total fixed costs
 
Break-even quantity   = total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
 
Margin of safety      = actual output − break-even quantity
Margin of safety (%)  = margin of safety ÷ actual output × 100
 
Target profit output  = (fixed costs + target profit)
                        ÷ contribution per unit
 
Target profit         = (contribution per unit × target output)
                        − fixed costs
 
Target price          = [(fixed costs + target profit) ÷ target output]
                        + variable cost per unit
```

## Profit and loss account

```
Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock
Gross profit       = sales revenue − cost of goods sold
Net profit before interest and tax
                   = gross profit − expenses
Net profit after interest and tax
                   = net profit before interest and tax − interest − tax
Retained profit    = net profit after interest and tax − dividends
```

## Balance sheet

```
Net fixed assets   = fixed assets − accumulated depreciation
Working capital    = current assets − current liabilities
 (also called net current assets)
Net assets         = total assets − total liabilities
Equity             = share capital + retained profit
Capital employed   = long-term liabilities + equity
 
Net assets = equity            ← always. Use it as your check.
```

## Ratios

```
Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ sales revenue × 100
Profit margin       = net profit before interest and tax
                      ÷ sales revenue × 100
Return on capital employed (ROCE)
                    = net profit before interest and tax
                      ÷ capital employed × 100
 
Current ratio       = current assets ÷ current liabilities
Acid test (quick)   = (current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities
```

Margins and ROCE are percentages. Liquidity ratios are plain numbers — write 1.6, not 160%.

```
HL only:
Gearing ratio       = long-term liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100
Stock turnover      = cost of goods sold ÷ average stock  (times/year)
                    = average stock ÷ COGS × 365          (days)
Debtor days         = debtors ÷ sales revenue × 365
Creditor days       = creditors ÷ cost of goods sold × 365
```

## Cash flow

```
Net cash flow    = total cash inflows − total cash outflows
Closing balance  = opening balance + net cash flow
Opening balance  = previous month's closing balance
```

## Investment appraisal

```
Payback period (even annual flows)
  = initial investment ÷ annual net cash flow
 
Payback period (uneven flows)
  = completed years + (amount still owed ÷ next year's inflow) × 12
    → answer in years and months
 
Average rate of return (ARR)
  = [(total returns − capital cost) ÷ years of use]
    ÷ capital cost × 100
```

```
HL only:
Net present value = Σ (discounted cash inflows) − capital cost
  (a discount table is printed in the exam paper)
```

## Marketing

```
Market share  = firm's sales ÷ total industry sales × 100
                (say whether by value or by volume)
Market growth = (this year's market size − last year's)
                ÷ last year's market size × 100
```

## Human resources and operations

```
Labour turnover = number of staff leaving in a period
                  ÷ average number employed × 100
 
Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of employees
```

```
HL on the formula sheet, but one-liners worth knowing:
Productivity rate        = total output ÷ total input × 100
Capacity utilization     = actual output ÷ productive capacity × 100
Defect rate              = defective units ÷ total units × 100
Reorder level, buffer stock — all HL production planning.
```

## Descriptive statistics (toolkit, SL)

```
Mean   = sum of values ÷ number of values
Median = middle value once ordered
         (even count → mean of the middle two)
Mode   = most frequent value
Range  = highest − lowest
```

Usually tested by interpretation, not calculation: the mean is distorted by outliers, so the median describes a skewed set better; a wide range or large standard deviation means the data is spread out and less safe to plan from.

# Part two — Definitions worth knowing cold

"Define the term" is worth 2 marks: 1 for partial understanding, 2 for a full definition. No application needed and examples earn nothing on their own — so give the general meaning, not an instance of it. Aim for one sentence with two ideas in it.

## Unit 1 — Introduction to business management

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Sole trader | A business owned and controlled by one person, who has unlimited liability for its debts. |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who share capital, decisions and profits, usually with unlimited liability. |
| Privately held company | An incorporated business with limited liability whose shares cannot be sold to the general public and can only be transferred with the other shareholders' agreement. |
| Publicly held company | An incorporated business with limited liability whose shares are traded freely on a stock exchange and whose accounts must be published. |
| Limited liability | Owners' losses are capped at the amount they invested; their personal assets cannot be taken to pay business debts. |
| Cooperative | A business owned and democratically run by its members, who share in the decisions and in any surplus, with each member having one vote. |
| Social enterprise | An organization that trades commercially but exists primarily to achieve a social or environmental objective, reinvesting its surplus into that purpose. |
| Non-governmental organization | A private, non-profit organization independent of government that operates to further a social, humanitarian or environmental cause. |
| Public sector | The part of the economy owned and run by the government, providing services such as education, health care and policing. |
| Primary sector | Business activity that extracts raw materials from the earth, such as farming, fishing and mining. |
| Secondary sector | Business activity that processes raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods. |
| Tertiary sector | Business activity that provides services rather than goods, such as retail, banking and transport. |
| Entrepreneur | An individual who organizes the factors of production and takes the financial risk of starting and running a business in pursuit of profit. |
| Vision statement | A statement of what an organization ultimately aspires to become or achieve in the long term. |
| Mission statement | A statement of an organization's core purpose — what it does, for whom, and why it exists. |
| Aim | A general long-term goal that gives an organization direction. |
| Objective | A specific, measurable target set to achieve an aim, usually within a stated time frame. |
| SMART objective | An objective that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. |
| Stakeholder | Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of an organization. |
| Internal stakeholder | A stakeholder who is a member of the organization, such as an employee, manager or shareholder. |
| Economies of scale | The fall in average cost per unit that a business achieves as it increases its scale of output. |
| Diseconomies of scale | The rise in average cost per unit that occurs when a business grows too large to manage efficiently. |
| Merger | Two businesses agreeing to combine to form a single new legal entity. |
| Acquisition / takeover | One business buying a controlling interest in another, which continues as part of the buyer. |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses creating a jointly owned separate entity to pursue a shared project, sharing costs, risks and profits. |
| Strategic alliance | An agreement between businesses to cooperate on a specific objective while remaining independent organizations. |
| Franchise | An arrangement in which a business sells the right to use its name, products and business model to another party in return for a fee and a share of revenue. |
| Multinational company | A business with production or service operations in more than one country, controlled from a headquarters in its home country. |

## Unit 2 — Human resource management

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Human resource planning | Forecasting an organization's future workforce needs in number and skill, and planning how to meet them. |
| Labour turnover | The rate at which employees leave an organization over a period, as a percentage of the average number employed. |
| Internal recruitment | Filling a vacancy with someone already employed by the organization. |
| Induction training | Training given to new employees to familiarize them with the organization, their role and its procedures. |
| On-the-job training | Training carried out at the workplace while the employee performs their actual job. |
| Appraisal | The formal assessment of an employee's performance against agreed targets, used to guide development and reward. |
| 360-degree feedback | An appraisal method in which feedback is gathered from a range of people around an employee — managers, peers, subordinates and sometimes customers. |
| Redundancy | Ending employment because the job itself is no longer needed, usually with compensation, rather than because of the employee's conduct. |
| Dismissal | Ending a person's employment because of their conduct or inability to do the job. |
| Organizational structure | The formal arrangement of roles, responsibilities and reporting relationships within an organization. |
| Span of control | The number of subordinates directly accountable to one manager. |
| Chain of command | The formal line of authority through which instructions pass from the top of an organization to the bottom. |
| Levels of hierarchy | The number of layers of authority in an organization, each with its own degree of responsibility. |
| Delegation | Passing authority for a task from a manager to a subordinate, while the manager keeps overall accountability. |
| Centralization | Concentrating decision-making authority in the hands of senior management at the top of the organization. |
| Delayering | Removing one or more levels of hierarchy to flatten the structure and cut costs. |
| Leadership | The ability to influence and inspire others towards achieving a shared goal. |
| Autocratic leadership | A style in which the leader makes decisions alone and expects them to be followed without consultation. |
| Democratic leadership | A style in which the leader consults employees and involves them in decision-making before deciding. |
| Laissez-faire leadership | A style in which the leader sets broad objectives and leaves employees largely free to decide how to achieve them. |
| Motivation | The internal and external factors that drive a person to put effort into their work and to keep doing so. |
| Financial reward | A monetary payment used to motivate, such as salary, wage, commission, performance-related pay or profit-related pay. |
| Performance-related pay | Additional payment linked to an employee meeting measured performance targets. |
| Job enlargement | Widening a job by adding more tasks at a similar level of responsibility. |
| Job enrichment | Deepening a job by adding more challenging tasks and greater responsibility or autonomy. |
| Job rotation | Moving employees between different tasks or roles to reduce monotony and build a wider skill set. |
| Teleworking | Working away from the employer's premises, usually from home, using communications technology to stay connected. |
| Flexitime | An arrangement in which employees work a set number of hours but choose, within limits, when to start and finish. |

## Unit 3 — Finance and accounts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Money spent on acquiring or improving fixed assets that will be used repeatedly over the long term. |
| Revenue expenditure | Money spent on the day-to-day running of a business, such as wages, utilities and stock. |
| Internal source of finance | Money raised from within the business itself, such as retained profit, personal funds or the sale of assets. |
| Retained profit | The portion of profit after interest, tax and dividends that is kept in the business for reinvestment. |
| Share capital | The money raised by a company from selling shares to its owners, which does not have to be repaid. |
| Overdraft | An arrangement allowing a business to withdraw more money than it holds in its bank account, up to an agreed limit. |
| Leasing | Paying a regular fee to use an asset owned by someone else, rather than buying it outright. |
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not change with the level of output, and is incurred even when nothing is produced. |
| Variable cost | A cost that changes in direct proportion to the level of output. |
| Direct cost | A cost that can be clearly traced to the production of a specific product or project. |
| Indirect cost (overhead) | A cost that cannot be attributed to any single product, but is incurred by the business as a whole. |
| Contribution | The amount each unit sold contributes towards covering fixed costs, calculated as price minus variable cost per unit. |
| Gross profit | The profit left after deducting the direct cost of producing the goods sold from sales revenue. |
| Current assets | Items owned by or owed to a business that are expected to be converted into cash within twelve months. |
| Current liabilities | Debts a business is due to repay within twelve months. |
| Liquidity | The ability of a business to convert assets into cash in order to pay its short-term debts as they fall due. |
| Working capital | The funds available for a firm's day-to-day operations, equal to current assets minus current liabilities. |
| Depreciation | The fall in the recorded value of a fixed asset over time, due to wear and tear or obsolescence. |
| Intangible asset | A non-physical asset of long-term value to a business, such as a patent, trademark, copyright or goodwill. |
| Goodwill | The extra value of a business above its net assets, arising from reputation, brand and customer relationships. |
| Trade credit | An arrangement to buy goods or services now and pay the supplier at an agreed later date. |
| Cash-flow forecast | A prediction of the money flowing into and out of a business over a future period, month by month. |
| Investment appraisal | The quantitative techniques used to assess whether a proposed investment is financially worthwhile. |
| Payback period | The time taken for an investment to generate enough net cash flow to recover its initial cost. |
| Average rate of return | The average annual profit of an investment expressed as a percentage of its initial cost. |

## Unit 4 — Marketing

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Marketing | The management process of identifying customer needs and satisfying them profitably. |
| Market orientation | An outward-looking approach in which a business researches customer needs first and designs its products around them. |
| Product orientation | An inward-looking approach in which a business focuses on making a high-quality product and assumes customers will follow, rather than researching the market first. |
| Market share | The proportion of total sales in a market, by value or volume, held by one business or product. |
| Market segmentation | Dividing a market into distinct groups of consumers with similar characteristics or needs. |
| Target market | The specific group of consumers a business aims its product and marketing at. |
| Niche market | A small, narrowly defined market segment with specific needs that a specialized product is designed to meet. |
| Mass market | A large, undifferentiated market at which a product is aimed with a single marketing approach. |
| Unique selling point | The feature of a product or business that distinguishes it from competitors in the minds of consumers. |
| Market positioning | How a product is perceived by consumers in relation to competing products. |
| Primary market research | The collection of original data directly from the market, for the specific purpose at hand. |
| Secondary market research | The use of data already collected by someone else for a different purpose. |
| Focus group | A small, selected group of consumers brought together to discuss their views on a product or idea in depth. |
| Random sampling | A method in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. |
| Quota sampling | A method in which the sample is divided into groups and a set number is selected from each, without random selection within them. |
| Branding | Creating a distinct identity — name, design, symbol — that distinguishes a product from competitors. |
| Brand loyalty | The tendency of consumers to keep buying the same brand rather than switching to a competitor. |
| Product life cycle | The stages a product passes through from launch to withdrawal: research and development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline. |
| Extension strategy | An action taken to prolong the maturity stage of a product's life cycle and delay its decline. |
| Cost-plus pricing | Setting price by adding a fixed mark-up or profit margin to the cost of producing each unit. |
| Penetration pricing | Setting a deliberately low price on launch in order to win market share quickly. |
| Price skimming | Setting a high initial price for a new product to recoup development costs, then lowering it over time. |
| Premium pricing | Setting a consistently high price to signal superior quality or exclusivity. |
| Loss leader | Pricing a product below cost to attract customers who will then buy other, profitable products. |
| Above-the-line promotion | Paid promotion through independent mass media, such as television, radio or press advertising. |
| Below-the-line promotion | Promotion the business controls directly, such as direct mail, sponsorship, sales promotions and point-of-sale displays. |
| Channel of distribution | The route a product takes from producer to final consumer, including any intermediaries. |
| E-commerce | The buying and selling of goods and services electronically, usually over the internet. |

## Unit 5 — Operations management

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Operations management | The business function responsible for turning inputs into finished goods or services. |
| Job production | Producing one unique item at a time, made to the individual requirements of a customer. |
| Batch production | Producing a group of identical items together, completing each stage for the whole batch before moving on. |
| Mass (flow) production | Producing large volumes of a standardized product continuously along a production line. |
| Cellular production | Organizing flow production into self-contained teams, each responsible for a complete stage of the process. |
| Outsourcing | Contracting a business function or process out to an external provider instead of carrying it out in-house. |
| Offshoring | Relocating a business function to another country, whether or not it is contracted out. |
| Reshoring | Bringing previously offshored operations back to the home country. |
| Break-even point | The level of output at which total revenue exactly equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss. |
| Margin of safety | The amount by which current or forecast output exceeds the break-even quantity. |
| Capacity utilization | The proportion of a firm's maximum possible output that it is currently producing. |
| Productivity rate | A measure of efficiency, expressing output produced relative to the inputs used to produce it. |
| Circular business model | A model designed to eliminate waste by reusing, repairing, refurbishing and recycling materials so resources stay in use. |

## The four key concepts

These run through the whole course and are worth being able to name in an evaluation: **creativity**, **change**, **ethics** and **sustainability**. Tying a judgement to one of them — the ethical cost of an option, or whether it's sustainable as the market changes — is an easy way to lift a conclusion above a straight cost comparison.
 
---

> **How a definition earns both marks.** One mark is for partial understanding, the second for a full definition. The pattern that works: name the category the term belongs to, then the feature that distinguishes it from everything else in that category. "A variable cost is a cost" gets you nothing; "a cost that changes in direct proportion to output" gets you two.
